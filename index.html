Neuroleptic malignant syndrome (NMS)

NMS is a rare, potentially fatal reaction to dopamine blockade: rigidity, fever, altered mental state and autonomic instability, usually within 2 weeks of starting or increasing an antipsychotic. Stop the drug, support, cool, and escalate early.

Definition and epidemiology

  • An idiosyncratic reaction to dopamine D2 antagonists, or to abrupt withdrawal of dopamine agonists (e.g. levodopa in Parkinson's disease).
  • Usually develops within the first 2 weeks of starting a drug or changing the dose, but can occur at any time.
  • Second-generation antipsychotics are not free of risk; most recent Australian adverse-reaction reports involve atypicals.
  • Incidence and mortality figures vary widely between series. Check: add the figures you want to quote from eTG or a current review.

Risk factors

  • Drug factors: high-potency first-generation antipsychotics, IM or depot routes, high doses, rapid titration, combining antipsychotics, co-prescribed lithium.
  • Patient factors: dehydration, agitation, physical restraint, exhaustion, previous NMS, catatonia, organic brain disease, iron deficiency.
  • Withdrawal type: stopping levodopa or other dopamine agonists abruptly (sometimes called parkinsonism-hyperpyrexia syndrome).
  • Remember non-psychiatric D2 antagonists: metoclopramide, prochlorperazine, droperidol, promethazine.

Pathophysiology

  • Hypothalamus: D2 blockade disrupts thermoregulation, causing fever.
  • Nigrostriatal pathway: D2 blockade causes lead-pipe rigidity, which generates heat and breaks down muscle (rhabdomyolysis, raised CK).
  • Sympathetic system: loss of dopaminergic inhibition causes autonomic lability (labile BP, tachycardia, sweating).
  • A second model proposes a primary skeletal-muscle defect similar to malignant hyperthermia. The two models are not mutually exclusive.

Diagnosis

There is no single test. The international expert consensus criteria (Gurrera et al., 2011) weight each feature with priority points out of 100. They are an aid to diagnosis and should not be used alone to rule NMS out.

eTG uses the DSM criteria: all three major criteria (dopamine antagonist exposure, severe muscle rigidity, hyperthermia) plus at least two of: tachycardia, hypertension, labile BP or sweating; raised CK or leucocytosis; dysphagia or tremor; altered conscious state, mutism or incontinence. eTG notes extrapyramidal features are always present and the classic tetrad includes temperature >39 °C.

Differential diagnosis

Serotonin toxicity, anticholinergic toxicity, malignant catatonia, malignant hyperthermia (after anaesthetic agents), CNS infection (meningitis, encephalitis including anti-NMDA receptor), heat stroke, sympathomimetic toxicity, alcohol or sedative withdrawal, thyroid storm. See the Comparison table.

Investigations

  • Bloods: CK (common finding is >3 × upper limit of normal; repeat serially), FBC (leucocytosis), UEC and creatinine, LFTs, calcium, magnesium, phosphate, iron studies (low serum iron), coagulation (DIC), blood gas (acidosis), TFTs.
  • Urine: myoglobin, drug screen.
  • To exclude other causes: blood cultures, CT brain and lumbar puncture if infection is possible, ECG.
  • Expected results in NMS: CT and MRI brain normal; CSF normal or slightly raised protein; EEG may show diffuse slowing (metabolic encephalopathy).

Management

  1. Stop the causative drug and anything that potentiates NMS (anticholinergics, lithium). If the cause is stopping a dopamine agonist, restart it.
  2. Escalate: medical team, HDU or ICU for moderate to severe cases, clinical toxicologist or NSW Poisons Information Centre 13 11 26.
  3. Airway: sedation, paralysis, intubation and ventilation may be needed in severe cases because of chest-wall rigidity.
  4. Circulation: IV fluids first-line for hypotension. Hyperthermic patients are usually volume depleted despite high BP and tachycardia.
  5. Hyperthermia (>39 °C or rapidly rising): severity correlates with mortality. Start rapid cooling urgently; cold IV fluids are first-line. Antipyretics have little effect.
  6. Sedation: non-drug approaches preferred. Small titrated doses of diazepam may help agitation. Antipsychotics (including droperidol) are absolutely contraindicated.
  7. Prevent complications: VTE prophylaxis, rhabdomyolysis (fluids, monitor CK and renal function), aspiration pneumonitis.
  8. Bromocriptine for severe or prolonged NMS, with a clinical toxicologist. No controlled-trial efficacy, but supported by case reports and experience.
  9. Severe and refractory: dantrolene or ECT, with a clinical toxicologist. ECT is also an option when catatonic features are prominent.

eTG dosing (adults): bromocriptine 2.5 mg orally or via nasogastric/orogastric tube every 8 hours, titrated to response (falling temperature and rigidity) up to 5 mg every 4 hours. Usually needed for 5–10 days. Paediatric dose not established.

Complications

Rhabdomyolysis and acute kidney injury, aspiration pneumonia, respiratory failure, DVT and PE, DIC, seizures, arrhythmias, death.

Rechallenge and prevention

  • About one-third of patients redevelop NMS when an antipsychotic is restarted (eTG). Seek psychiatrist advice on whether and how to restart.
  • Wait until fully recovered (at least 2 weeks after resolution is a common recommendation), use a lower-potency drug from a different class, start low, titrate slowly, avoid depots, keep hydrated, and monitor temperature, vitals and CK.
  • Low to moderate quality evidence suggests NMS does not recur with clozapine after an episode, which supports its use when needed.
  • Document the reaction clearly and report it to the TGA.
  • Prevention: avoid dehydration, avoid rapid IM escalation in agitated, exhausted or restrained patients, and recognise early rigidity and fever.

Serotonin toxicity

Serotonin toxicity is a dose-related spectrum of excess CNS serotonin, not an idiosyncratic reaction. It starts within hours of a dose change or new combination. Diagnose with the Hunter criteria, stop the serotonergic drugs, and treat severe cases (temperature >39 °C or rapidly rising, rigidity with sustained clonus) as a medical emergency.

Definition and terminology

  • "Serotonin toxicity" is preferred to "serotonin syndrome", because it is a spectrum from mild to life-threatening.
  • It occurs in three settings: adverse reaction at therapeutic doses (usually mild to moderate), overdose of one agent (usually moderate), and drug interactions (almost all severe cases).
  • Serotonin toxicity occurs in about 15% of SSRI overdoses, but single-agent SSRI overdose rarely causes severe toxicity.
  • Severe toxicity almost always comes from combining drugs that act at different sites, most commonly an MAOI plus a serotonin reuptake inhibitor.

Causes

MechanismExamples
Reuptake inhibitionSSRIs (incl. vortioxetine, dapoxetine), SNRIs (venlafaxine, desvenlafaxine, duloxetine, atomoxetine, reboxetine), clomipramine, imipramine, St John's wort. Opioids: high risk dextromethorphan, tramadol, pethidine; lower risk methadone, tapentadol
MAO inhibitionPhenelzine, tranylcypromine, moclobemide, linezolid, methylene blue, isoniazid, lamotrigine (listed by eTG)
Serotonin releaseAmphetamines, metamfetamine, phentermine, MDMA, cocaine, cathinones; all opioids occasionally
SynthesisL-tryptophan
Receptor agonistsLSD, 2C-substituted phenylethylamines (e.g. NBOMe), lithium

Methylene blue is a potent MAO-A inhibitor. Combining it with an SSRI, SNRI or TCA can cause severe toxicity (relevant in surgery, sepsis and vasoplegia).

Pathophysiology

Excess serotonin in the CNS overstimulates postsynaptic receptors. The life-threatening effects (rigidity, hyperthermia) are mediated mainly by 5-HT2A receptors, which is the basis for using 5-HT2A antagonists.

Clinical features

DomainFeatures
NeuromuscularHyperreflexia, clonus (ankle, ocular, spontaneous), myoclonus, tremor, shivering, hypertonia or rigidity. Lower limbs more affected than upper.
AutonomicHyperthermia (severe: >39 °C or rapidly rising), tachycardia, sweating, flushing, mydriasis, diarrhoea, hyperactive bowel sounds
Mental stateAnxiety, agitation, hypomania, confusion

Diagnosis: Hunter Serotonin Toxicity Criteria

In a patient who has taken a serotonergic agent, ANY ONE of:

  1. Spontaneous clonus.
  2. Inducible clonus AND (agitation OR diaphoresis).
  3. Ocular clonus AND (agitation OR diaphoresis).
  4. Tremor AND hyperreflexia.
  5. Hypertonia AND temperature >38 °C AND (ocular OR inducible clonus).

The Hunter criteria (sensitivity 84%, specificity 97%) were developed at the Hunter Area Toxicology Service in Newcastle, NSW. They are more specific than Sternbach's criteria, whose features overlap with anticholinergic delirium, withdrawal states and SSRI discontinuation. The key bedside step is a focused neurological exam: tone, reflexes, clonus (including ocular) and pupils.

Severity

  • Mild: tremor, mild tachycardia, inducible clonus, lower-limb hyperreflexia. Often occurs at therapeutic doses and rarely troubles the patient.
  • Moderate: agitation, sustained clonus, tachycardia, temperature <39 °C. Distressing; needs symptomatic relief.
  • Severe (serotonergic crisis): rapidly progressive hyperthermia, rigidity with sustained clonus, seizures. Progresses to multi-organ failure within hours if untreated. An MAOI or stimulant is often involved.

Delirium is not prominent, which helps separate it from anticholinergic toxicity. Symptoms usually resolve within 12–24 hours of stopping the drugs.

Differential diagnosis

The key distinguishing sign is neuromuscular excitation (hyperreflexia, clonus, myoclonus). It is absent in NMS, anticholinergic delirium, CNS infection and malignant hyperthermia. Sympathomimetic toxicity can share hyperreflexia and tremor, but lacks sustained clonus. eTG also lists withdrawal syndromes (alcohol, baclofen, benzodiazepines, GHB) and non-convulsive seizures as mimics. See the Comparison table.

Investigations

  • Mild: UEC, CK, glucose, venous blood gas, ECG.
  • Moderate to severe: add FBC, LFTs, calcium, coagulation.
  • In overdose: paracetamol level, ECG for QTc (e.g. citalopram, escitalopram) and QRS (TCAs).

Management

  1. Stop all serotonergic drugs.
  2. Observe: at least 6 hours for moderate toxicity; 12 hours after slow-release preparations such as venlafaxine XR.
  3. Mild: supportive care only.
  4. Moderate, distressed: oral diazepam first. If no improvement, add cyproheptadine (oral 5-HT2A antagonist). No controlled trials, but used successfully in practice.
  5. Moderate, cannot take oral: IV diazepam or midazolam. IV chlorpromazine is an alternative, but causes postural hypotension and can be fatal if the diagnosis is actually NMS.
  6. Severe: urgent clinical toxicologist advice and ICU. Rapid cooling for rising temperature, with cold IV fluids first-line (patients are usually volume depleted). Sedate for rigidity and sustained clonus; intubation, ventilation and paralysis may be needed. Treat seizures as for poisoning.
  7. Avoid: antipyretics (no effect), dantrolene (no evidence), bromocriptine (may worsen toxicity), and physical restraint (increases heat and lactic acidosis).

eTG dosing (adults):

DrugDose
Diazepam oral5–20 mg; repeat after 30 min if needed. Usually ≤120 mg in 24 h
Cyproheptadine oral12 mg single dose. For long half-life or modified-release drugs (fluoxetine, MR tramadol): 8 mg three times daily until resolved (usually within 24 h)

Benzodiazepine-tolerant patients may need higher doses. Paediatric doses are in eTG.

Complications

Rhabdomyolysis, acute kidney injury, metabolic acidosis, hyperkalaemia, liver failure, DIC, seizures, death.

Restarting and prevention

  • Most patients recover fully within 24–72 hours. Reintroduce serotonergic treatment only after resolution, ideally one agent at a time at a lower dose.
  • Avoid MAOI combinations. Isbister et al. suggest this alone would prevent most life-threatening toxicity.
  • Respect washout periods when switching to or from an MAOI (fluoxetine has a long half-life); use Psymatik for switching schedules. Tyramine restrictions continue for 14 days after stopping phenelzine or tranylcypromine (see MAOI).
  • Watch for non-psychiatric serotonergic drugs: tramadol, fentanyl, pethidine, dextromethorphan, linezolid, methylene blue, triptans (low risk).
  • Report to the TGA.

Lithium toxicity

Lithium has a narrow therapeutic index and is cleared almost entirely by the kidneys, so anything that lowers kidney function can tip a stable patient into toxicity. Chronic toxicity is the most common and most dangerous pattern, and can be severe at only modestly raised levels. Treat the patient, not the number.

Pharmacology relevant to toxicity

  • Lithium behaves like sodium: distributed through total body water, not protein-bound, and eliminated by the kidneys.
  • Usual half-life is about 12 hours, and much longer in renal impairment.
  • Lithium moves into and out of the CNS slowly. Acute overdose can look well despite a high level, chronic toxicity causes neurotoxicity at lower levels, and neurological recovery lags behind the fall in serum level (days to weeks).
  • eTG lists 0.5–1.0 mmol/L as therapeutic. The ISBD/IGSLI consensus maintenance target is 0.6–0.8 mmol/L (0.4–0.6 if good response but poor tolerance, or in older adults; 0.8–1.0 if insufficient response and well tolerated).

Patterns of toxicity

PatternSettingTypical picture
ChronicOn long-term lithium; level rises over days to weeks (most common)Neurological features dominate, often at modest levels. Worst prognosis. Often triggered by dehydration, AKI or a new interacting drug.
AcuteLithium-naive patient takes an overdoseNausea, vomiting, diarrhoea; tremor, hyperreflexia, ataxia. Saturable absorption and slow CNS uptake mean <25 g rarely causes major toxicity unless kidneys are impaired. A level >5 mmol/L may not mean severe poisoning; a level still high at 24 h carries greater risk.
Acute-on-chronicOn lithium and takes an extra amountManaged as acute poisoning (eTG). Mixed GI and neurological features; nephrogenic DI can potentiate toxicity.

Precipitants of chronic toxicity

  • Volume loss: vomiting, diarrhoea, fever, poor intake, heat, low-salt diet.
  • Reduced kidney function: AKI, CKD, nephrogenic diabetes insipidus (common on long-term lithium) with inability to drink enough.
  • Drugs: ACE inhibitors, angiotensin receptor blockers, NSAIDs, thiazide and loop diuretics; any recent dose increase or new medicine.
  • Patient factors (eTG): age over 50, previous lithium toxicity, intercurrent illness, thyroid dysfunction.

Clinical features

  • Renal: nephrogenic DI causes polyuria (obligatory water loss 3–12 L/day), dehydration and hypernatraemia, which in turn worsen toxicity.
  • Lithium can contribute to serotonin toxicity when combined with serotonergic drugs.

SILENT

The syndrome of irreversible lithium-effectuated neurotoxicity: lasting neurological damage after lithium toxicity, mainly cerebellar (ataxia, dysarthria, nystagmus), sometimes with cognitive impairment or movement disorders. Recovery from neurotoxicity may take weeks and can be incomplete, so early treatment matters.

Investigations

  • Serum lithium: in acute or acute-on-chronic poisoning, every 6 hours to find the peak and confirm elimination (serum levels do not reflect CNS or total body load). In chronic toxicity, repeat until clearly falling. Note the time of the last dose.
  • Creatinine and urea.
  • Serum sodium: every 6–12 hours if conscious state is altered, to assess hydration and detect hypernatraemia.
  • TFTs (chronic toxicity).
  • ECG.
  • Calcium, glucose, venous blood gas. In overdose: paracetamol level and co-ingestant screen.
  • Nephrogenic DI clues: thirst and drinking >5 L/day, polyuria and nocturia, sodium at the upper end of normal, urine osmolality low relative to serum.

Management

  1. Stop lithium and all nephrotoxic or interacting drugs (ACE inhibitors, ARBs, NSAIDs, diuretics).
  2. Escalate: NSW Poisons Information Centre 13 11 26 or a clinical toxicologist; nephrology early if haemodialysis may be needed.
  3. Airway: manage urgently if reduced consciousness compromises airway or breathing.
  4. Fluids: IV sodium chloride 0.9% is first-line for dehydration and enhances lithium elimination when kidneys work. Patients with nephrogenic DI need larger-than-usual volumes to match high urine output. Watch sodium closely.
  5. Seizures: check sodium and treat nephrogenic DI. Self-limiting seizures need no treatment; persistent or recurrent seizures get an IV benzodiazepine.
  6. Decontamination (acute ingestion): activated charcoal is not recommended (it does not bind lithium). For ingestions >50 g, consider whole bowel irrigation within 4 hours, with a clinical toxicologist. The patient must protect their airway or be intubated; do not use if aspiration risk or ileus; check hourly for distension.
  7. Haemodialysis: see below.
  8. Disposition, acute: admit if haemodialysis or treatment of neurotoxicity is needed. Discharge if asymptomatic after 6–12 hours of observation with serial levels <1.5 mmol/L and falling.
  9. Disposition, chronic: admit if there is neurotoxicity (e.g. coma, seizures). Lithium does not need restarting immediately; consider stopping or reducing the dose, as recurrence risk is high.
  10. Advise no driving or operating machinery for at least 3 days after discharge (chronic toxicity).

eTG, acute and acute-on-chronic: haemodialysis is rarely needed with normal kidney function and only after discussion with a clinical toxicologist. Consider it for severe toxicity (especially neurotoxicity such as coma or seizures), lithium >4.0 mmol/L with kidney impairment, or lithium >5.0 mmol/L. Continue until clinical improvement and level <1.0 mmol/L, or at least 6 hours if levels are not readily available.

eTG, chronic: not usually needed if kidney function is normal. Indicated if the level is expected to still be >1.0 mmol/L at 36 hours, or there are features of severe toxicity. eTG uses a nomogram (Buckley et al. 2020) plotting eGFR against the presenting level to predict the 36-hour level; discuss with a clinical toxicologist. Even low-intensity CRRT at least doubles lithium clearance.

In all cases, monitor the level after dialysis for rebound.

Anticholinergic toxicity

Anticholinergic toxicity is an agitated delirium with hot, dry skin, dilated pupils, tachycardia and urinary retention. The priority is controlling the delirium: physostigmine for severe cases from a pure anticholinergic, otherwise sedation with a benzodiazepine or droperidol.

Causes

GroupExamples
Pure anticholinergicsAtropine, benzatropine, oxybutynin, hyoscine butylbromide
Drugs with anticholinergic effectsTCAs, sedating antihistamines, antipsychotics (low-potency agents, clozapine, olanzapine, quetiapine)
PlantsBrugmansia (angel's trumpet), Datura stramonium (jimsonweed), Atropa belladonna, Duboisia (corkwood)

In psychiatric settings, think of cumulative anticholinergic burden in older adults (e.g. benzatropine plus a TCA plus an antipsychotic), not only overdose.

Pathophysiology

Competitive blockade of muscarinic acetylcholine receptors. Peripheral blockade causes the autonomic signs; central blockade causes delirium.

Clinical features

DomainFeatures
PeripheralDry skin and mouth, flushing, mydriasis, tachycardia, urinary retention, ileus (reduced bowel sounds, distension), raised temperature (especially children)
CNSConfusion, mainly visual hallucinations, picking at sheets or imaginary objects, mumbling or incoherent speech, agitation, aggression, delirium, sedation, occasionally seizures

Mnemonic: hot as a hare, blind as a bat, dry as a bone, red as a beet, mad as a hatter, full as a flask.

Differential diagnosis

Dry skin and absent bowel sounds separate it from serotonin and sympathomimetic toxicity (both sweaty). Normal tone and no clonus separate it from NMS and serotonin toxicity. Also consider CNS infection, alcohol or sedative withdrawal, and other causes of delirium.

Investigations

  • ECG: QRS and QTc, especially if a TCA or antihistamine may be involved.
  • Bladder scan for retention.
  • UEC, CK, glucose, venous blood gas; paracetamol level in overdose.
  • Exclude other causes of delirium as clinically indicated.

Management

  1. Supportive care in a low-stimulus environment with close observation. Mild cases may need observation only.
  2. Catheterise acute urinary retention. This can settle agitation and avoid sedation.
  3. Mild to moderate, cooperative: oral diazepam.
  4. Severe, pure anticholinergic: physostigmine is the first-line antidote. It reverses delirium (which also helps confirm the diagnosis) and allows later sedation to be oral. Give in a resuscitation area. Avoid in bradycardia or bronchospasm. Short-acting, so repeat doses are often needed after 1–2 hours.
  5. Mixed agents (TCA, antihistamine): consult a clinical toxicologist before physostigmine, because seizures and arrhythmias have been reported.
  6. If physostigmine is unavailable: parenteral sedation with droperidol or a benzodiazepine. Large IV doses may be needed. Droperidol is contraindicated if the cause is antipsychotic toxicity or NMS is possible.
  7. Restraint: avoid if possible; only briefly and alongside sedation.

Physostigmine is not registered in Australia. It is available through the Special Access Scheme; check your state's emergency and life-saving drugs register for hospitals that hold stock.

TCA poisoning

TCA overdose kills through sodium-channel blockade, coma and seizures, and deteriorates fast: effects appear within 2 hours and most serious complications occur within 6. Call a clinical toxicologist or NSW Poisons Information Centre 13 11 26 early. Key treatments are early intubation, activated charcoal and serum alkalinisation with sodium bicarbonate.

Drugs

Amitriptyline, clomipramine, dosulepin, doxepin, imipramine, nortriptyline.

Risk assessment by dose

DoseExpected effect
<5 mg/kgUsually non-toxic
5–20 mg/kgSedation and anticholinergic effects
>20 mg/kgSevere toxicity: coma, seizures, major cardiovascular toxicity

Asymptomatic at 6 hours means toxicity is unlikely. Acidosis (from respiratory depression, seizures or reduced consciousness) worsens all effects.

Clinical features

  • CNS: rapidly falling consciousness, respiratory depression, coma; seizures in severe poisoning (linked to QRS widening).
  • Cardiovascular: hypotension (alpha blockade, myocardial depression), sinus tachycardia, QRS widening and arrhythmias.
  • Anticholinergic: mydriasis, warm dry skin, dry mouth, urinary retention, reduced bowel sounds. Anticholinergic delirium can persist 24–48 hours after recovery and becomes more obvious as sedation wears off.

ECG changes

  • Sinus tachycardia.
  • QRS widening: the wider the QRS, the higher the risk of ventricular arrhythmias and seizures.
  • Dominant R wave in aVR (>3 mm, R:S ratio about 2:1): sensitive predictor of arrhythmias and seizures.
  • Broad-complex tachycardia, with P waves lost in the preceding T wave.
  • QT prolongation accompanies QRS widening but is not clinically significant; torsades is not usually seen.

Investigations

  • ECG on arrival and serially: continuous monitoring and 12-lead ECGs every 2 hours for at least 6 hours if conscious state is altered.
  • Blood gas to monitor acidosis and alkalinisation.
  • Serum potassium during alkalinisation (bicarbonate causes hypokalaemia).
  • Paracetamol level and co-ingestant screen.

Management

  1. Escalate early: clinical toxicologist or 13 11 26; ICU for severe poisoning.
  2. Intubate and ventilate if ingestion >20 mg/kg, or there is falling consciousness, seizures, hypotension or progressive ECG changes. Ideally within 2 hours of ingestion. Hand-ventilate during induction to avoid apnoea and worsening acidosis; consider a dose of sodium bicarbonate at induction.
  3. Activated charcoal for ingestions >5 mg/kg if alert, cooperative, able to protect airway and within 2 hours; any time via NG or OG tube if intubated.
  4. Serum alkalinisation if QRS widening is progressive with airway, breathing or circulatory compromise (arrhythmias, hypotension) or CNS depression: IV sodium bicarbonate plus hyperventilation, targeting pH 7.45–7.55. Bicarbonate is also the antiarrhythmic of choice.
  5. Hypotension: IV fluids first; noradrenaline if persistent.
  6. Seizures: usually brief and self-limiting but signal severe toxicity. Persistent or recurrent seizures: IV benzodiazepine and intubation.
  7. Avoid class 1A antiarrhythmics (quinidine, procainamide). Follow ALS protocols for arrest, with toxicologist advice.
  8. Disposition: admit if symptomatic; discharge if asymptomatic for at least 6 hours. Advise no driving or operating machinery for at least 3 days.

Sympathomimetic toxicity is adrenergic excess: agitation, tachycardia, hypertension, hyperthermia and sweating. Benzodiazepines are the cornerstone, treating agitation, tachycardia, hypertension and seizures together. In psychiatry it presents as stimulant-related agitation or psychosis, or as toxicity from ADHD medicines, MAOIs or SNRIs.

Differential diagnosis

Serotonin toxicity (sustained clonus, often a serotonergic co-ingestant), anticholinergic toxicity (dry skin, absent bowel sounds), alcohol or sedative withdrawal, thyroid storm, CNS infection, primary psychosis or mania. Investigations

  • ECG and troponin if chest pain or ECG changes.
  • UEC, potassium, glucose, CK, venous blood gas (lactate).
  • CT brain if focal signs, severe headache or reduced consciousness.
  • Paracetamol level if overdose is possible.

Management

  1. Sedation: IV benzodiazepines for severe agitation, also treating tachycardia and hypertension. Large doses are often needed. Oral diazepam may be enough for a cooperative, mildly agitated patient.
  2. If benzodiazepines fail in severe agitation with signs of excited delirium: droperidol.
  3. Hyperthermia (>39 °C or rapidly rising): rapid cooling urgently; cold IV fluids first-line (patients are usually volume depleted).
  4. Persistent hypertension despite sedation: IV glyceryl trinitrate. If maximum rate is reached, ask a clinical toxicologist about other agents (e.g. beta blockers).
  5. Seizures: IV benzodiazepines at larger doses than for sedation. Other anticonvulsants are often ineffective.
  6. Complications: manage ACS, dissection, stroke, arrhythmias and rhabdomyolysis on their own pathways.

Stimulant poisoning specifics

  • Which toxidrome: metamfetamine is mainly adrenergic and dopaminergic (sympathomimetic toxidrome); MDMA is mainly serotonergic (can present as serotonin toxicity).
  • Toxic doses: more than 1 g of cocaine or metamfetamine is potentially lethal. Dexamfetamine, methylphenidate and phentermine <1 mg/kg, and pseudoephedrine <15 mg/kg, are unlikely to cause toxicity. Purity and adulterants make illicit doses unpredictable.
  • PMA and PMMA (found as MDMA contaminants) can cause fatal hyperthermia and intracranial haemorrhage.
  • Hyponatraemia (especially MDMA) from SIADH plus excess water intake; severe cases cause cerebral oedema and seizures. Fluid restriction corrects most within 24–48 h.   • Other complications: pneumothorax, pneumomediastinum.

QTc prolongation and torsades de pointes

Drug-induced torsades de pointes (TdP) is rare, but psychiatric patients carry many risk factors at once. A QTc >500 ms or a rise >60 ms from baseline marks high risk. Treat it by removing the drug, correcting potassium and magnesium, and giving IV magnesium for TdP. No QTc value is an absolute bar to a needed psychotropic; weigh cardiac risk against psychiatric risk.

Definitions

  • QT: start of QRS to end of T wave, measured in the lead with the longest interval using the tangent (maximum slope) method. Include large U waves fused to the T wave.
  • Prolonged QTc: >440 ms in men, >460 ms in women (LITFL). APA uses Bazett-based bands: men normal <430, borderline 431–470, prolonged >470; women normal <450, borderline 451–480, prolonged >480.
  • High risk of TdP: QTc >500 ms, or an increase >60 ms from baseline.
  • Correction formula: Bazett over-corrects above 100 bpm and under-corrects below 60 bpm. Use Fridericia (QTcF) when heart rate is outside 60–100 bpm, and use the same formula for serial comparisons.
  • In overdose: risk is better judged by the absolute QT plotted against heart rate on the QT nomogram (Chan, Isbister et al. 2007).
  • Wide QRS: QT lengthens without a true repolarisation problem (bundle branch block, pacing, TCAs). Measure manually and interpret with care.

Torsades de pointes

Polymorphic VT with QRS complexes "twisting" around the baseline. Usually comes in self-limiting bursts causing palpitations, dizziness or syncope, but can degenerate into VF and sudden death. Think of it in any patient on QT-prolonging drugs with new syncope, palpitations, seizure or cardiac arrest.

Risk factors

Non-modifiableModifiable

eTG also lists: age 70 or older, history of syncope or palpitations, hypothyroidism, significant bradycardia, family history of sudden cardiac death or long QT, and baseline QTc above the upper limit of normal (440 ms males, 460 ms females). Risk is cumulative: a single non-cardiac drug rarely causes TdP on its own. QT prolongation is more likely with IV antipsychotics (e.g. for acute behavioural disturbance).

Monitoring

  • Antipsychotics (eTG): ECG before starting, unless therapy is expected to last less than 24 hours. If not possible in an acutely unwell patient, do it as soon as practical. If at risk, repeat once at steady state (after 3–5 half-lives), and again after dose increases or starting or stopping an interacting drug. Routine ECG annually on antipsychotics.

  • Check potassium, magnesium and calcium in anyone with vomiting, diarrhoea, eating disorders, alcohol use disorder, diuretics or PPIs.
  • No ECG available should not by itself stop a needed psychotropic; weigh the known risks.

Management of QTc prolongation

  1. Baseline QTc >500 ms before an antipsychotic (eTG): correct electrolytes, treat hypothyroidism, heart failure or bradycardia, refer suspected congenital long QT to a cardiac electrophysiologist, and stop or reduce other QT-prolonging drugs. Avoid high-risk agents (haloperidol, ziprasidone); choose a low-risk agent (aripiprazole). Regular ECGs and electrolytes; consider cardiology review.
  2. During treatment, QTc >500 ms or rise >60 ms (eTG): address modifiable factors, then reduce the dose or switch to a lower-risk antipsychotic. If impossible or ineffective, continue with regular ECGs and electrolytes and consider cardiology review.
  3. Admit for telemetry during drug withdrawal if there is syncope, ECG instability (ventricular ectopy, T-wave alternans, AV block, QRS widening), QTc >500 ms or a rise ≥60 ms (UpToDate), with a defibrillator immediately available.
  4. Correct electrolytes: keep potassium in the high-normal range (about 4.5–5.0 mmol/L) and replace magnesium (first, if both are low).
  5. Symptoms (syncope, palpitations, dizziness): urgent medical review and cardiac monitoring.

Management of torsades de pointes

  1. Initial: continuous cardiac monitoring, IV access, 12-lead ECG, assess haemodynamic stability.
  2. Pulseless or unstable: defibrillate (ALS) with IV magnesium.
  3. IV magnesium sulfate for all TdP, even if serum magnesium is normal and even if the QT does not shorten. Give more slowly when there is a pulse, as rapid infusion can cause hypotension and asystole.
  4. Stop all QT-prolonging drugs; correct potassium to the high-normal range.
  5. Recurrent TdP despite magnesium: temporary transvenous overdrive pacing at about 100 bpm (preferred), with isoprenaline as a bridge to pacing. Isoprenaline worsens polymorphic VT that is not long-QT related, so confirm the diagnosis.
  6. Avoid antiarrhythmics that prolong repolarisation; lidocaine is reserved for refractory cases.

Myocarditis (Ronaldson protocol)

  • Baseline: troponin I or T, CRP, ECG, echocardiogram where available.
  • Monitoring: troponin and CRP on days 7, 14, 21 and 28 (then weekly for 2 more weeks and 6-monthly per the ACSQHC chart), plus daily temperature, pulse and BP.
  • Mild rise in troponin or CRP, persistent tachycardia, or infective symptoms: daily troponin and CRP until settled; clozapine can usually continue.

  • Admit if confirmed; cardiology review and echo. Fever in the first 4 weeks needs urgent FBC and cardiac enzymes.
  • Eosinophilia can accompany myocarditis.
  • Rechallenge after myocarditis is a specialist decision with cardiology.

Gastrointestinal hypomotility

Clozapine combines antimuscarinic and 5-HT3 blockade, slowing the whole gut. Complications include faecal impaction, ileus, ischaemic bowel, perforation and sepsis; mortality is reported at 15–28%.

  • Prevention: document baseline bowel habit; check bowel habit daily for 4 weeks then weekly; start an osmotic laxative (macrogol) with clozapine; add a stimulant (senna) if no bowel action for 48 hours; remove other anticholinergics; fibre, fluids, exercise.
  • Emergency: abdominal pain, distension, vomiting, absent bowel sounds or overflow diarrhoea → withhold clozapine, urgent abdominal X-ray or CT, surgical review, IV fluids and nil by mouth as directed.
  • Ask about bowels at every review; patients often do not report constipation.

Seizures

  • Dose and level related; risk rises with high doses (e.g. >600 mg/day), rapid titration and high plasma levels.
  • Manage the seizure as usual. Then check the clozapine level, arrange EEG and neurology review, consider anti-seizure medicine (valproate is contraindicated in women of childbearing potential; lamotrigine is an alternative), withhold clozapine for 1 day and restart at half the previous dose (ACSQHC).
Check: your vault note suggests routine valproate or lamotrigine prophylaxis and EEG monitoring. The ACSQHC guidance is to consider anti-seizure medicine after a seizure or with high levels, not routinely.

Smoking, levels and toxicity

  • Tobacco smoke (not nicotine) induces CYP1A2 and lowers clozapine levels substantially (by about 50%). Stopping smoking, including admission to a smoke-free unit, can cause levels to rise within days. Nicotine replacement does not prevent this. eTG notes the same effect for olanzapine and haloperidol.
  • Other causes of rising levels: infection or inflammation, CYP1A2 inhibitors (fluvoxamine, ciprofloxacin), caffeine changes.
  • Toxicity features: sedation, hypersalivation, confusion or delirium, myoclonus, seizures, hypotension, tachycardia.
  • Action: record smoking status at every review and notify the medical officer of any change. eTG recommends therapeutic drug monitoring, where available, to guide the lowest effective dose: check a trough level (12 hours post-dose) before and after any change in smoking, and reduce the dose as levels and symptoms indicate.
  • Restarting after >48 hours off: psychiatric review first, then restart at 12.5 mg once or twice daily and re-titrate (risk of hypotension, collapse and seizures).
Check: eTG gives no fixed dose reduction on stopping smoking. If you want a figure on the site, cite your local clozapine protocol or the Maudsley guidelines.

Acute dystonia and akathisia

Acute dystonia is a frightening, sometimes airway-threatening muscle spasm that starts within minutes to days of a new or increased dopamine blocker. It responds within minutes to parenteral benzatropine. Akathisia is the commonest acute movement side effect to be mistaken for agitation, and giving more antipsychotic makes it worse.

Acute dystonia

  • What it is: sustained or brief muscle contraction causing twisting movements or abnormal postures, often of the face, neck and trunk.
  • Forms: torticollis, trismus, tongue protrusion, oculogyric crisis (fixed upward gaze), opisthotonus, and laryngeal or pharyngeal dystonia (stridor, dysphagia, breathlessness), which is an airway emergency.
  • Who and when: most common in young males; within minutes to days of starting or increasing an antipsychotic, or starting or stopping an interacting drug. Also caused by antiemetics (metoclopramide, prochlorperazine, droperidol).
  • Highest-risk drugs (eTG Table 8.31): haloperidol (+++); flupentixol, zuclopenthixol (++ to +++); chlorpromazine (++); asenapine, lurasidone (+ to ++). Lowest: clozapine (–), olanzapine and quetiapine (– to +).
  • Differentials: seizure, tetanus, hypocalcaemia, functional (dissociative) disorder, stroke. Dystonia is usually painful, conscious and responds rapidly to an anticholinergic.

Management of acute dystonia

  1. Laryngeal dystonia: treat as an airway emergency. Give parenteral benzatropine immediately, with oxygen and airway support; call for help.
  2. Benzatropine IV or IM as below; expect relief within minutes. Repeat once after 20 minutes if no response.
  3. Then switch to oral benzatropine for up to 2 weeks to prevent recurrence, tapering over the last few days.
  4. Adjust therapy: stop or reduce other dopamine antagonists (e.g. metoclopramide), reduce the antipsychotic dose, or switch to an agent less likely to cause extrapyramidal effects.
  5. Explain what happened; acute dystonia is distressing and affects future adherence. Document it as an adverse reaction.

Akathisia

  • What it is: an unpleasant inner restlessness with an urge to move (pacing, rocking, shifting weight). Usually early in treatment or after a dose increase.
  • Why it matters: often misread as worsening psychosis or agitation, leading to more antipsychotic. It is linked to distress, non-adherence and, in some reports, aggression and suicidality.
  • Management (eTG): reduce the antipsychotic dose until it settles, or switch to an agent less likely to cause extrapyramidal effects.   • NMS: see NMS; rigidity with fever and altered consciousness is not dystonia.

Valproate emergencies

Valproate causes four emergencies: overdose (sedation to coma, with multi-organ toxicity at high doses), hyperammonaemic encephalopathy (which occurs at normal levels and normal liver function), hepatotoxicity, and pancreatitis. It is also highly teratogenic. Any new drowsiness, confusion, vomiting or ataxia on valproate needs a serum ammonia, not just a valproate level.

Hyperammonaemic encephalopathy (at therapeutic doses)

  • What it is: valproate inhibits the urea cycle (carbamoylphosphate synthetase-I) and depletes carnitine. Ammonia rises, brain glutamine increases and astrocytes swell, causing cerebral oedema.
  • Can occur with normal valproate levels and normal LFTs. Ammonia levels correlate poorly with severity.
  • Features: falling consciousness, lethargy, vomiting, cognitive slowing, confusion, ataxia, increased seizure frequency, focal neurological signs. EEG shows generalised slowing, sometimes triphasic waves.
  • Risk factors: topiramate or phenobarbital co-prescription, urea cycle disorders (e.g. ornithine transcarbamylase deficiency), liver disease, carnitine deficiency, fasting or strict vegetarian diet, intellectual disability, long-term treatment.
  • Management: stop valproate (and topiramate); check ammonia, LFTs and valproate level; exclude other causes of delirium; give carnitine (oral or IV 50–100 mg/kg/day in case series, or the eTG regimen above if severe); seek neurology or toxicology advice. Symptoms, EEG and ammonia usually normalise after withdrawal. Screen for a urea cycle disorder if it recurs or is severe.
  • In psychiatric settings, VHE is easily mistaken for sedation, worsening mood disorder or catatonia.

Hepatotoxicity and pancreatitis

  • Hepatic dysfunction occurs in more than 1%; fatal liver failure is rare and usually in the first 6 months. Highest risk: children under 2, polytherapy, metabolic or mitochondrial disease (avoid valproate in suspected mitochondrial disorders). Routine LFTs do not reliably predict liver failure, so warn patients about symptoms (malaise, anorexia, vomiting, jaundice, facial oedema). Stop valproate if hepatotoxicity is suspected; carnitine may help in severe cases.
  • Pancreatitis occurs in less than 0.1% but can be fatal and can occur after years of use. Abdominal pain, nausea, vomiting or anorexia need prompt review and a lipase. Stop valproate if confirmed.
  • Other serious effects: thrombocytopenia or altered platelet function (spontaneous bruising or bleeding needs review), SJS/TEN and DRESS, polycystic ovary syndrome, male infertility.

Pregnancy and women of childbearing potential

  • RANZCP 2020: sodium valproate should be avoided in women of childbearing age (recommendation 10.7). Valproate and carbamazepine should be avoided in pregnancy.
  • Valproate carries the highest risk among antiepileptics of major congenital malformations (including neural tube defects) and poor neurodevelopmental outcomes. Australian pregnancy category D.
  • If there is no alternative: effective contraception, documented discussion of risk, the lowest effective dose (avoid ≥700 mg/day if possible), and high-dose folic acid (5 mg daily) before conception and in the first trimester. Do not stop abruptly after conception without specialist advice.
  • Pregnant women on antiepileptic drugs can be referred to the Australian Pregnancy Register.

Monitoring and interactions

  • Before starting: LFTs, FBC with platelets, pregnancy status and contraception, weight. Avoid in liver disease or a family history of it, urea cycle disorders and porphyria; reduce the dose in renal impairment.
  • During treatment: measure ammonia for lethargy, vomiting or ataxia; FBC for bruising or bleeding; LFTs and lipase for symptoms. Levels help with suspected toxicity or adherence; take them at least 8 hours after the last dose.
  • Key interactions: valproate roughly doubles lamotrigine levels (SJS risk; halve lamotrigine dosing); carbapenems sharply lower valproate levels (risk of relapse or seizures); topiramate increases hyperammonaemia; carbamazepine, phenytoin and phenobarbital lower valproate levels; large-dose aspirin raises them; valproate lowers olanzapine levels.
Check: neither RANZCP 2020 nor the Psych Scene bipolar guide sets a routine valproate monitoring schedule in text, and Australian Prescriber says regular monitoring is not required for most patients (epilepsy context). Add your service's schedule for bipolar disorder (commonly LFTs and FBC at baseline, in the first 6 months and when symptomatic, plus levels during titration) if you want one on the site. The "halve lamotrigine" advice is standard but not in these sources; confirm against the lamotrigine product information.

Malignant catatonia

Malignant catatonia is catatonia with autonomic instability: fever, labile BP, tachycardia and tachypnoea. It can be fatal within days. Stop antipsychotics, give high-dose lorazepam, and move to urgent ECT if there is no rapid response.

Definition and relationship to NMS

  • Catatonia is a psychomotor syndrome ranging from stupor to severe excitement. It can co-occur with delirium or coma.
  • "Malignant" means clinically significant autonomic abnormalities (temperature, BP, heart rate, respiratory rate).
  • Malignant catatonia was described long before antipsychotics existed. Many authors see NMS as a drug-induced form of the same syndrome, sharing reduced dopaminergic function in basal ganglia circuits.

Causes

  • Psychiatric: bipolar disorder (more often than major depression), schizophrenia, other psychotic disorders.
  • Medical: autoimmune encephalitis (including anti-NMDA receptor), systemic lupus erythematosus, CNS infection, metabolic disturbance, non-convulsive status epilepticus.
  • Drug-related: antipsychotic exposure, abrupt benzodiazepine or other GABAergic withdrawal (including clozapine withdrawal).

Diagnosis

  • DSM-5: at least 3 of 12 signs: stupor, catalepsy, waxy flexibility, mutism, negativism, posturing, mannerisms, stereotypy, agitation, grimacing, echolalia, echopraxia.
  • Bush-Francis Catatonia Rating Scale: adds signs such as mitgehen, gegenhalten, ambitendency, automatic obedience and verbigeration. Use it to screen and to track response.
  • Malignant features: fever, tachycardia, labile BP, tachypnoea, sweating, reduced consciousness.
  • Lorazepam challenge: lorazepam 1–2 mg IV (or IM or sublingual), reassess with the Bush-Francis scale after 15–30 minutes. Rapid improvement supports the diagnosis; no response does not exclude it.

Investigations

  • CK, FBC, UEC, LFTs, iron studies, TFTs, glucose, calcium, blood gas, drug screen.
  • Look for medical causes: EEG (non-convulsive status), MRI brain, lumbar puncture with autoimmune encephalitis antibodies when the picture fits.
  • Distinguish from NMS by history (antipsychotic exposure, timing) and from serotonin and anticholinergic toxicity by examination (see the Comparison table).

Management

  1. Stop antipsychotics and other dopamine blockers (including antiemetics such as metoclopramide). They are contraindicated in malignant catatonia and raise NMS risk, especially with dehydration, parenteral or rapidly escalating doses, or previous catatonia.
  2. Escalate: ICU for malignant catatonia (hyperthermia, severe hypertension, arrhythmias from electrolyte imbalance).
  3. Start lorazepam and prepare for ECT at the same time (consent or legal authorisation, anaesthetic and medical review). If there is a substantive response within 24–48 hours, lorazepam can continue in place of ECT. If not, start ECT and stop the benzodiazepine. Mortality rises if ECT is delayed beyond about 5 days from onset.
  4. Lorazepam dosing: do not withhold doses for sedation alone unless there is respiratory depression. Watch for respiratory depression with opioids or other sedatives; flumazenil reverses it.
  5. Supportive care: IV fluids, cooling, nutrition (enteral if needed), VTE prophylaxis (DVT occurs in more than 25%, highest in retarded catatonia), pressure care with 2-hourly repositioning, aspiration precautions, bowel and bladder care, passive range of motion, monitor CK and renal function.
  6. Treat the cause: e.g. immunotherapy for autoimmune encephalitis, restarting a withdrawn benzodiazepine or clozapine.
  7. After remission: continue oral lorazepam at the effective dose for 3–6 months, then taper. Keep it going if an antipsychotic is reintroduced for the underlying illness; reintroduce cautiously and only once eating, drinking and moving normally.
  8. Refractory, or ECT unavailable or declined: case reports support memantine, amantadine (may worsen psychosis), zolpidem, carbamazepine, topiramate, valproate or rTMS.

ECT

  • Indication: first-line for malignant catatonia; also for non-malignant catatonia not responding to a benzodiazepine within about 1 week. RANZCP lists catatonia and NMS as established indications.
  • Response: in a small case review, 9 of 11 patients with malignant catatonia responded to ECT versus 2 of 5 to a benzodiazepine. Overall, benzodiazepines, ECT or both achieve recovery in about 60–80% of catatonia.
  • Technique (UpToDate): bitemporal placement and brief pulse are preferred. Usually three times a week; in malignant catatonia, daily until physiologically stable (often 2–5 treatments). Give at least 6 treatments to reduce sudden relapse; most remit within 6–12. If response plateaus, continue ECT and restart the same benzodiazepine, then check ECT quality and re-examine the diagnosis.
  • Anaesthesia: use a non-depolarising muscle relaxant (e.g. rocuronium) in immobile patients with muscle damage, because suxamethonium can cause hyperkalaemia. Flumazenil before ECT may be used to reverse benzodiazepine anticonvulsant effect.
  • Consent: follow the relevant mental health legislation where capacity is absent or the patient is involuntary.

Complications

Dehydration, malnutrition, aspiration pneumonia, DVT and PE, pressure injuries, faecal impaction, urinary retention, rhabdomyolysis and AKI, contractures. Malignant catatonia can leave lasting cognitive deficits, apathy or limb contractures; mortality may reach 20% (renal failure, thromboembolism, sepsis). Non-malignant catatonia generally has a good prognosis, especially with mood or toxic-metabolic causes; it can persist for years in schizophrenia and may recur.
